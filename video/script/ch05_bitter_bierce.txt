This is where Bierce becomes BIERCE. In 1881, he takes over a San Francisco magazine called The Wasp. Which is the perfect name for anything he's involved in. A few years later, a young newspaper owner named William Randolph Hearst hires him to write a column for the San Francisco Examiner.

Back then, there's no radio, no TV, no internet. Newspapers were the internet. And Bierce was Hearst's star attack dog. People called him "Bitter Bierce." Think of the most brutal critic online today, then hand him the biggest megaphone in the West. Everybody read him. A lot of people were scared of him.

His columns became his most famous book: a fake dictionary called The Devil's Dictionary. And since this is a history channel, I have to share this one:

"History: an account mostly false, of events mostly unimportant, which are brought about by rulers mostly knaves, and soldiers mostly fools."

Okay. Rude. But he was a soldier, so he'd know.

And in his spare time? He writes war stories and horror stories. The most famous one is "An Occurrence at Owl Creek Bridge." Spoiler warning. During the Civil War, a man is about to be hanged from a railroad bridge. The rope snaps. He falls into the creek, dodges the bullets, and runs all day and all night to get home to his wife. He reaches out to hug her...

And he's dead. The rope never snapped. The whole escape happened in his head, in the second before he died.

It later aired as an episode of The Twilight Zone, and Kurt Vonnegut called it the greatest American short story. Notice what it's about: that blurry line between being here and being gone. Bierce kept coming back to it his whole life.
